PASTA: A Scalable Framework for Multi-Policy AI Compliance Evaluation

Imagine you're developing an AI system. The problem is, there are all these different AI policies you're supposed to follow, and each one comes with pages and pages of complicated requirements. Unless you're a policy expert or you go hire one, it's pretty hard to figure out if you're following the rules.

So we built PASTA, which functions like an AI lawyer for developers. And it is open-source.

You fill in a form describing your AI system, and PASTA goes through different policies to figure out which requirements you're meeting, which ones you might not be, and what you could do about it. The idea is to make something that normally takes a lot of time and expertise much easier for everyday developers.

How it works.

The bottom logic of PASTA is really just large text comparing powered by LLM, and how do we achieve such with efficiency and accuracy. To solve this issue, we first structure different policies into the same granular format. We ensemble a model card structure for AI systems applying model cards. Then we perform pairwise comparison. To achieve efficiency while balancing accuracy, we mastered prompt engineering and applied algorithmic strategies. The below storyboard demonstrates how PASTA works:

Challenges.

PASTA evaluates 23 AI system documentation sections against N policy articles. That's O(N) per AI system, which sounds manageable until you realize each operation is an LLM request that costs time and money. The challenge is to minimize LLM calls without sacrificing accuracy.

I designed two algorithmic strategies to address this:

  • Irrelevancy filtering: Skip policy articles that don't apply to certain documentation section,
  • Dynamic chunking: Group relevant policy text in chunks per request to reduce unnecessary calls while preserving important context.

Of course there are other challenges such as standardizing the heterogeneous policies and ensembling a comprehensive enough but easy to fill out model card format for system input. Please read the paper for these.

Reflection.

I hesitate to place the responsibility of AI governance entirely on developers. Many AI practitioners are unaware of the risks their systems pose or the regulations they must follow. They believe that AI safety remains a distant concern and is disconnected from their everyday work.

I see three opportunities to improve responsible AI practices. 1, AI safety and compliance should be integrated into education. 2, compliance should be incorporated early in the development process. Regulatory barriers found after months of development are costly. Compliance should be an ongoing practice that allows AI practitioners to adjust their products throughout development. 3, making 2 possible requires more accessible and affordable compliance/audit resources. Lightweight, inexpensive, open-source tools like PASTA could be part of a potential solution.

There is a great deal of room between writing a policy and expecting everyone to comply. Regulations become less respected when poorly enforced and work against its abiders when inaccessible. Taking into consideration developers' capacity to comply and whether these policies actually reach them, I suspect much of the meaningful work lies in that rather unglamorous middle ground of making existing regulations more accessible.
